This note follows the draft manuscript *Fusions of the point–distance schemes of generalized hexagons
and octagons, and the orbital algebra of the Tits group on 1755 points*.<Note>The statements below are copied from the manuscript. The hexagon proof is the manuscript's; the octagon proof is summarised, not reproduced. The research context is in §2, [Fusions of generalized polygon association schemes](/research/generalized-polygon-schemes).</Note>

## The result

A distance-regular graph $\Gamma$ has the intersection array of a <Gloss t="polygon">generalized $2d$-gon</Gloss> of order
$(s,t)$ when $b_0=s(t+1)$, $b_i=st$ and $c_i=1$ for $1\le i\le d-1$, and $c_d=t+1$. Its distance
relations $A_0,\dots,A_d$ form a symmetric association scheme.

<Theorem n="B" name="fusions of generalized hexagons and octagons" pencil="the main one">
Let $d\in\{3,4\}$ and let $\Gamma$ be a distance-regular graph with the intersection array of a
generalized $2d$-gon of order $(s,t)$, where $s,t\ge2$ are integers. The distance scheme
$\{A_0,\dots,A_d\}$ has a proper fusion scheme if and only if $s=t$. In that case the fusion is
unique, namely $\{\,A_0,\ A_1+\dots+A_{d-1},\ A_d\,\}$, and it is strongly regular.
</Theorem>

<Corollary n="C">
Every thick finite generalized octagon has $s\neq t$ (Feit–Higman). Hence the distance scheme of its
point graph has no proper fusion. In particular the $5$-class scheme of Theorem A has no proper
fusion: apart from the trivial partition of $\{R_1,\dots,R_4\}$ into one block and the partition into
singletons, no partition of the nontrivial orbitals into blocks gives an association scheme, and no
strongly regular graph is a union of orbitals.
</Corollary>

Theorem A is the orbital algebra of the Tits group on $1755$ points, set out in
[Note 2](/mathematics/tits-1755).

## The fusion criterion

Let $(X,\{R_i\}_{i=0}^D)$ be a symmetric association scheme with adjacency matrices $A_i$,
valencies $k_i$ and primitive idempotents $E_0=\tfrac1{|X|}J,E_1,\dots,E_D$. The eigenmatrix
$P=(P_{ej})$ is defined by $A_j=\sum_e P_{ej}E_e$. A *fusion* is a partition
$\Lambda=\{B_1,\dots,B_r\}$ of $\{1,\dots,D\}$ such that
$V_\Lambda=\operatorname{span}\{I,A_{B_1},\dots,A_{B_r}\}$, $A_B=\sum_{j\in B}A_j$, is closed under
multiplication; then $(X,\{R_0,R_B\})$ is an $r$-class association scheme. It is *proper* if
$2\le r\le D-1$.

For $e,f\in\{1,\dots,D\}$ and $B\subseteq\{1,\dots,D\}$ put

$$
\delta^{ef}(B)=\sum_{j\in B}\bigl(P_{ej}-P_{fj}\bigr).
$$

Since $\sum_{j=0}^DA_j=J$ and $JE_e=0$ for $e\ge1$, we have $\sum_{j=1}^DP_{ej}=-1$ and therefore

$$
\delta^{ef}(B^c)=-\delta^{ef}(B). \tag{1}
$$

The following criterion is standard (cf. Bannai–Ito); the manuscript includes a proof.

<Lemma n={1} name="fusion criterion">
For a partition $\Lambda$ of $\{1,\dots,D\}$ into $r$ blocks let $e\sim_\Lambda f$ mean
$\delta^{ef}(B)=0$ for all $B\in\Lambda$. This is an equivalence relation on $\{1,\dots,D\}$ with at
least $r$ classes, and $\Lambda$ is a fusion if and only if it has exactly $r$ classes.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Write $A_B=\sum_e u_B(e)E_e$ with $u_B(e)=\sum_{j\in B}P_{ej}$. Let $\approx$ be the equivalence
relation on $\{0,\dots,D\}$ with $e\approx f$ iff $u_B(e)=u_B(f)$ for all $B\in\Lambda$, and let
$F\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{D+1}$ be the algebra of functions constant on $\approx$-classes; $F$ is
identified with $\operatorname{span}\{\sum_{e\in K}E_e: K\ \text{a class}\}$. Then
$V_\Lambda\subseteq F$ and $\dim V_\Lambda=r+1$, because the $A_B$ have disjoint supports. Since
$J=I+\sum_BA_B\in V_\Lambda$ equals $|X|E_0$, the class of $0$ is $\{0\}$, so $\approx$ has $1+c$
classes, where $c$ is the number of classes of $\sim_\Lambda$ on $\{1,\dots,D\}$, and $1+c\ge r+1$.

If $c=r$ then $V_\Lambda=F$, which is closed under multiplication. Conversely suppose $V_\Lambda$ is
closed under multiplication. If $e\not\approx f$ choose $x\in V_\Lambda$ with $x(e)\ne x(f)$.
Polynomials in $x$ lie in $V_\Lambda$ (it contains $I$), so $V_\Lambda$ contains the indicator
function of $\{g:x(g)=x(e)\}$; multiplying such indicators over finitely many separating elements
gives the indicator of the $\approx$-class of $e$. Hence $F\subseteq V_\Lambda$, $V_\Lambda=F$ and
$1+c=r+1$.
</Proof>

<Lemma n={2}>
Let $\Lambda$ be a proper fusion.

(a) If $D=3$ then $r=2$, and $\Lambda=\{B,B^c\}$ is a fusion iff $\delta^{ef}(B)=0$ for at least one
pair $\{e,f\}$.

(b) If $D=4$ and $r=2$, then $\Lambda=\{B,B^c\}$ is a fusion iff $\delta^{ef}(B)=0$ for at least two
distinct pairs.

(c) If $D=4$ and $r=3$, then $\Lambda=\{\{a\},\{b\},\{c,d\}\}$ and a fusion requires a pair $\{e,f\}$
with $\delta^{ef}(\{a\})=\delta^{ef}(\{b\})=0$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
By Lemma 1 and (1) the conditions $\delta^{ef}(B)=0$ for all blocks of a two-block partition reduce
to one block. (a) The relation $\sim_\Lambda$ has at least $2$ classes on three points, so it has
exactly $2$ iff some pair is related. (b) A two-class relation on four points has class sizes $(2,2)$
or $(3,1)$, hence at least two related pairs; conversely two related pairs give at most two classes.
(c) Three classes on four points means one related pair $\{e,f\}$; then
$\delta^{ef}(\{a\})=\delta^{ef}(\{b\})=0$, and $\delta^{ef}(\{c,d\})=0$ follows from (1).
</Proof>

<Lemma n={3}>
If the scheme is metric (the point scheme of a distance-regular graph), the numbers
$P_{e1}=\theta_e$ are pairwise distinct, so $\delta^{ef}(\{1\})\ne0$ for all $e\ne f$. In particular
no proper fusion has $\{1\}$ (or $\{1\}^c$) as a block.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
$A_1$ generates the Bose–Mesner algebra, which has dimension $D+1$, so $A_1$ has $D+1$ distinct
eigenvalues.
</Proof>

## The eigenmatrix of a hexagon

For $d=3$ the valencies are $k_1=s(t+1)$, $k_2=s^2t(t+1)$ and $k_3=s^3t^2$, and $a_i=s-1$ for
$1\le i\le 2$, $a_3=(s-1)(t+1)$. Put $m=\sqrt{st}$.

<Proposition n={4}>
The eigenvalues of $A_1$ are $s(t+1)$, $-t-1$ and $s-1\pm m$. The eigenmatrix $P=(P_{ej})$, rows
$e=0,\dots,3$ and columns $j=0,\dots,3$, is

$$
\footnotesize P=\begin{pmatrix}
1 & s(t+1) & s^2t(t+1) & s^3t^2\\
1 & s-1+m & ms-m-s & -ms\\
1 & s-1-m & -ms+m-s & ms\\
1 & -t-1 & t(t+1) & -t^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
The quotient (intersection) matrix of $A_1$ is tridiagonal with entries $b_{j-1},a_j,c_{j+1}$; its
characteristic polynomial is $(x-s(t+1))(x+t+1)(x^2-2(s-1)x+(s-1)^2-st)$. For each root $\theta$ the
row $(P_{ej})_j$ is determined by $P_{e0}=1$, $P_{e1}=\theta$ and
$\theta P_{ej}=b_{j-1}P_{e,j-1}+a_jP_{ej}+c_{j+1}P_{e,j+1}$. The manuscript verifies symbolically,
modulo $m^2=st$, that the displayed rows satisfy this recursion and the sum rule $\sum_jP_{ej}=0$
($e\ge1$); the verification is reproduced by the accompanying script.
</Proof>

## Proof for hexagons

<Proof of="Theorem B for d = 3">
Let $s,t\ge2$ and $m=\sqrt{st}\gt 0$. By Lemma 2(a) we must decide, for
$B\in\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}$, whether some pair $\{e,f\}\subset\{1,2,3\}$ has $\delta^{ef}(B)=0$.
From Proposition 4:

*$B=\{1\}$.* Impossible by Lemma 3 (directly: the differences are $2m$, $s+t+m$, $s+t-m$, and
$m\lt s+t$).

*$B=\{2\}$.* The column $j=2$ is $(ms-m-s,\ -ms+m-s,\ t(t+1))$. Then $\delta^{12}=2m(s-1)\gt 0$ and
$\delta^{23}=-m(s-1)-s-t(t+1)\lt 0$. If $\delta^{13}=0$ then $m(s-1)=s+t^2+t$; squaring and using
$m^2=st$ gives $f(s,t)=0$ with

$$
\begin{aligned}f(s,t)&=t\,s^3-(2t+1)s^2\\
&\qquad-t(2t+1)s-t^2(t+1)^2 .\end{aligned}
$$

This factors as $f(s,t)=(s^2+st+t^2)\bigl(st-(t+1)^2\bigr)$. The first factor is positive, so
$s=(t+1)^2/t=t+2+1/t$, which is not an integer for $t\ge2$. (For real $t\gt 0$ this point does
satisfy $m(s-1)=s+t^2+t$, with $m=t+1$, so the condition is not vacuous over the reals.)

*$B=\{3\}$.* The column $j=3$ is $(-ms,\ ms,\ -t^2)$. Here $\delta^{12}=-2ms\ne0$ and
$\delta^{23}=ms+t^2\gt 0$, while $\delta^{13}=t^2-ms=0$ iff $t^4=m^2s^2=s^3t$ iff $s=t$.

So a proper fusion exists iff $s=t$, and then only for $\Lambda=\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}$.

*Strong regularity.* For $s=t=q$ the eigenvalues of $A_1+A_2$ are, by Proposition 4,
$k=q(q+1)(q^2+1)$ on $E_0$, $q^2-1$ on $E_1,E_3$ and $-q^2-1$ on $E_2$. A graph with exactly these
eigenvalues is strongly regular with $\mu=k+rs'$ and $\lambda=\mu+r+s'$, where $r=q^2-1$,
$s'=-q^2-1$, giving

$$
\begin{gathered}\Bigl(\tfrac{q^6-1}{q-1},\ q(q+1)(q^2+1),\\
q^3+q^2+q-1,\ (q+1)(q^2+1)\Bigr).\end{gathered}
$$
</Proof>

<Remark n={5}>
These are the parameters of the collinearity graph of $Q(6,q)$. For the split Cayley hexagon $H(q)$
the identification is classical: its points are all points of $Q(6,q)$ and two points are at
distance $\le2$ in $H(q)$ iff they are collinear in $Q(6,q)$. The computation above shows that the
parameter identity holds for any distance-regular graph with the array of a hexagon of order
$(q,q)$. Direct computation of the intersection numbers for $q=2,3,4,5$ gave $(63,30,13,15)$,
$(364,120,38,40)$, $(1365,340,83,85)$, $(3906,780,154,156)$.
</Remark>

## Octagons

For $d=4$ put $m=\sqrt{2st}$. The eigenvalues of $A_1$ are $s(t+1)$, $-t-1$, $s-1\pm m$ and $s-1$.
Every difference $\delta^{ef}(B)$ then has the form $\alpha+\beta m$ with $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[s,t]$,
and the manuscript lists all $42$ cases (seven two-block partitions, six pairs) in an appendix, with
the sign of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for $s,t\ge2$. Its proof for octagons goes through those cases in
three steps: two-block partitions, where the only proper fusion is $\{\{1,2,3\},\{4\}\}$ and it
occurs iff $s=t$; three-block partitions, of which none is a fusion; and strong regularity. For $s=t$
the eigenvalues of $A_4$ are $k_4=s^7$ on $E_0$, $-s^3$ on $E_1,E_3$ and $s^3$ on $E_2,E_4$, hence
$A_4$ is strongly regular with parameters

$$
\Bigl(\tfrac{s^8-1}{s-1},\ s^7,\ s^7-s^6,\ s^7-s^6\Bigr).
$$

The full case analysis is in Section 5 and Appendix A of the manuscript; it is not reproduced here.

<Proof of="Corollary C">
For a thick finite generalized octagon of order $(s,t)$ the Feit–Higman theorem requires $2st$ to be
a perfect square, so $s=t$ is impossible ($2s^2$ is never a square). Theorem B gives no proper
fusion. A strongly regular graph that is a union $\bigcup_{i\in S}R_i$ of orbitals would give a
two-block fusion $\{S,S^c\}$, which does not exist.
</Proof>

## How sharp it is

The hypothesis $s,t\ge2$ cannot be dropped. Direct computation from the intersection numbers shows
that the thin octagon schemes of order $(1,2)$ and $(1,8)$ admit the fusions $\{\{2\},\{1,3\},\{4\}\}$
and $\{\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\}$, and that of order $(2,1)$ admits $\{\{2\},\{3\},\{1,4\}\}$ and
$\{\{3\},\{1,2,4\}\}$; the scheme of order $(8,1)$ has none. For order $(2,1)$ the graph of class $3$
is strongly regular with parameters $(45,16,8,4)$, those of the triangular graph $T(10)$. For $s=t$
the octagon fusion $\{\{1,2,3\},\{4\}\}$ is formal: it would give strongly regular graphs such as
$(255,128,64,64)$ for $s=2$ and $(3280,2187,1458,1458)$ for $s=3$, but no octagon of order $(s,s)$
exists.

## Verification

Everything was recomputed in exact arithmetic by a verification script, including a direct
test, from the intersection numbers alone, of the closure condition for every proper partition for
all $841$ integer pairs $2\le s,t\le30$ and $d=3,4$; it found proper fusions exactly on the diagonal
$s=t$ and only the fusion named in Theorem B. An independent exact certificate avoids
eigenvalues and square roots: it writes the closure conditions of every proper partition as
polynomial equations in $\mathbb{Q}[s,t]$, reduces them with a lexicographic Gröbner basis over
$\mathbb{Q}$, and isolates the real solutions exactly. For all real $s,t\ge1$ the outcome agrees with
Theorem B and with the thin cases above.
